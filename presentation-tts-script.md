Welcome to the presentation on the design and characterization of a precision motion system, developed as a project for the course Design of Precision Engineering.

The presentation is divided into two independent sections. The first section focuses on the design of a precision motion de-amplifier. The second section focuses on the static calibration of an existing precision motion amplifier.

The first part concerns the design of the precision motion de-amplifier.

Starting with the design activity, this slide summarizes the workflow followed during the design phase. The process started from the customer need. From there, the product was defined and the requirements were translated into engineering specifications using Q F D. Then, the system was decomposed into atomic functions, which also helped improve our understanding of precision motion mechanisms. Different concepts were generated using morphological combination and Triz, compared through robust decision-making, and the selected concept was developed into an engineering model.

In the product definition phase, the main customer needs were identified. The mechanism had to provide straight input and output motion, with a de-amplification ratio of one to twenty. The input-output relation had to be highly linear, with a maximum deviation of five percent, and the motion transmission had to be reversible. The customer request also included constraints on input stroke, input force, space envelope, sensing surface, and support through a Kelvin clamp.

After defining the product, Q F D was used to organize the customer request and translate it into design information. Customer needs were first expressed as requirements, in a form that is still understandable from the customer point of view. These requirements were then translated into specifications, which are more directly usable in engineering design. The Q F D methodology will not be explained in detail here, since it was already presented in a previous lecture.

The next step was product decomposition. Existing systems were analyzed and decomposed into their atomic functions. This work was performed independently by each group member, so that different examples and interpretations could be considered. Product decomposition was useful not only for identifying the basic functions of the system, but also for improving our understanding of the topic. In the example shown, the system was first analyzed as a whole, considering its degrees of freedom and motion behavior. It was then split into elementary functions associated with specific components.

Starting from the system requirements and from the knowledge obtained through product decomposition, a functional architecture was derived. This architecture clarifies which functions the system must perform, independently from the specific physical solution. The diagram is organized hierarchically, starting from the main function and then moving toward more specific functions. The blue blocks correspond to higher-level functions, while the white blocks correspond to more elementary ones. For example, the frame function is split into providing the preload force and avoiding misalignment between the input and output directions.

After the functional decomposition, a morphological map was designed. This map describes, at a conceptual level, the connections between the different atomic functions. For example, the unidirectional translation is strictly related to the reversible gain of one to twenty, while the input and output systems are connected to the unidirectional translation. The map also shows that, to obtain a reversible system, the architecture should be symmetric from the point of view of the atomic functions. However, this map does not represent the final physical architecture. Its purpose is to identify how the functions are related before implementation.

Triz, the Theory of Inventive Problem Solving, was used as a second method for concept generation. Its purpose is to solve engineering contradictions. The process starts by identifying a contradiction, then abstracting it into a more general problem. The forty Triz inventive principles are then used to suggest possible solution strategies, which must finally be translated back into a practical implementation. In this project, Triz was applied to contradictions between system requirements.

This slide shows one example. The real problem was to improve the reversibility of the input-output relation while preserving small envelope dimensions. In the Triz domain, this became a more general contradiction. The suggested principle was number thirteen, "the other way round". In this case, it was interpreted as structural symmetry. Therefore, if the mechanism is structurally symmetric, inverse functioning is allowed without changing the overall system dimensions.

In the concept generation phase, the previous analyses were used to generate possible concepts. Product decomposition provided a knowledge base, while the morphological map helped translate atomic functions and their connections into conceptual solutions. At this stage, the focus was mainly on system architecture rather than implementation details. The number of concepts was intentionally balanced: large enough to explore different engineering solutions, but limited enough to maintain a clear overview and make comparison manageable.

The first concept shown is a single-stage mechanical solution based on gears. In this case, the motion transmission and the required gain are obtained directly through the gear ratio. This solution is simple and easy to understand, but it is not ideal for a high-precision application because gear mechanisms introduce backlash, friction, and contact-related errors.

The next concepts are based on compliant mechanisms and use a multi-stage architecture. Here, motion is transmitted through elastic deformation, which provides high repeatability and avoids backlash and friction. Some stages guide the rectilinear motion through structural geometric symmetry, while another stage provides amplification or de-amplification through lever mechanisms. Another important aspect is the symmetry between the input and output sides, which enables reversible operation.

The following concepts are also compliant mechanisms, but with a single-stage architecture. The objective is to integrate motion transmission and de-amplification in the same mechanism, instead of separating them into multiple stages. In particular, there is only one lever stage, implemented through levers working in parallel. These concepts still rely on flexure hinges, elastic deformation, lever effects, and structural geometric symmetry.

This slide presents two additional compliant-mechanism concepts based on a lever cascade. In these concepts, motion amplification is obtained through several levers arranged in series. Also in this case, they rely on the same kinematic and compliant principles as the multi-stage architectures.

After generating the concepts, a robust decision-making process was used to compare them. The system requirements were adopted as decision criteria, and belief maps were used by each group member to evaluate the capability of each concept to satisfy them. The results were then collected in a decision matrix, where each criterion was assigned a weight and each concept received a score. Based on this process, Concept II was selected. It uses several compliant lever stages in cascade to obtain the required motion amplification, and it showed the best overall satisfaction value.

After the selection, the concept was developed into a more detailed model. The modeling phase started from the kinematic design, where the main motion behavior and the lever-based architecture were defined. Then, dynamic design and material selection were developed in parallel, because the elastic behavior depends on both geometry and material properties. Finally, these choices were verified through simulations.

From a kinematic point of view, the mechanism is based on a lever architecture. The levers provide the required linear displacement de-amplification: a larger input displacement produces a smaller output displacement with the target ratio. The same architecture supports reversible behavior because the input and output sides are arranged symmetrically. Straight-line motion is ensured by structural symmetry, which helps guide the motion and reduce unwanted rotations.

The dynamic design was mainly based on elastic deformation. In compliant mechanisms, motion is obtained through elastic deformation instead of conventional mechanical joints. As a result, backlash and friction are not present, and the system can achieve high repeatability and reversible behavior. Flexure hinges were adopted to implement the joints. Different hinge geometries were analyzed through analytical models and parametric studies. The final design uses both leaf-spring hinges and circular hinges, combining compliance, precision, and rotational capability.

Material selection was supported by the Ashby map shown in the slide. Since the mechanism works through elastic deformation, high yield strength was a key requirement. The material had to allow the flexure hinges to deform without entering the plastic region. For this reason, Beryllium-Copper was selected, because it provides a suitable combination of elastic behavior and high yield strength.

The simulation results were compared with the original design requirements. The output-input plot shows that the simulated behavior is very close to the ideal linear interpolation. At the maximum input displacement of ten millimeters, the mechanism reaches the required gain of one to twenty. The maximum deviation from linearity is about zero point seven percent, well below the allowed five percent. The space envelope is also within the required limits. The only value that does not satisfy the original target is the maximum input force, which is about one point zero seven newtons instead of zero point one newtons. However, this force is still small in absolute terms, and the overall performance remains positive.

The second simulation slide shows the frequency response. At low frequency, the system behaves as an attenuator, consistently with the de-amplification function. A resonance appears at about thirty radians per second, corresponding to approximately four point seven seven hertz. After this resonance, the magnitude decreases strongly, showing post-resonance attenuation, and the phase plot shows a phase delay after resonance.

The next part introduces the characterization and static calibration of the precision motion amplifier.

This activity is independent from the previous design work. The objective is to calibrate an existing precision motion system and characterize the static relation between input and output displacements.

The measured system was mounted on a passive vibration-isolation bench, in order to reduce external disturbances. It was positioned through a Kelvin-clamp precision mounting, with a tension spring used for attachment. The input displacement was applied manually through a precision screw, while input and output displacements were measured using LIF sensors.

Before performing the calibration, the effect of noise was evaluated. The plots show the sensor drift and the stochastic noise contribution for both input and output measurements. The sensor drift is on the order of units of nanometers per second, while the stochastic contribution is on the order of tens of nanometers, with a ninety-five percent confidence level. This contribution was considered negligible because the operating displacements are much larger: on the order of millimeters for the input and hundredths of millimeters for the output.

For the static calibration, a second-order model was adopted to fit the measured data. The model includes an offset term, a direction-dependent term for forward and backward motion, a linear coefficient, and a quadratic coefficient. The table reports the estimated coefficients and standard errors. The dominant term is the linear coefficient, while the quadratic contribution is very small. Residual normality was checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test, giving a p-value of zero point fifteen. The maximum deviation from linearity is equal to zero point zero one percent.

The calibration conclusions are summarized by the two plots. On the left, the input-output relation is shown for both backward and forward motion. The points are very close to the fitted line, confirming the high linearity of the calibration model. On the right, the residuals are plotted as a function of the output displacement. Their magnitude is very small, and the forward and backward measurements remain close to each other. This confirms limited hysteresis effects and a highly linear input-output relation.
